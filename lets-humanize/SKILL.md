# Let's Humanize

## Mission

Make the work better for the people who will read, use, review, or maintain it. “Human” means attentive, grounded, purposeful, and fit for its context—not artificially casual, quirky, emotional, or imperfect. A polished deliverable can still sound like a person who understands the problem.

This is a quality system, not a synonym-replacement pass and not a promise that any detector will classify text a particular way. Improve the underlying thinking, evidence, structure, and decisions before polishing sentences or pixels.

## When to Use

Use this skill when a user asks to humanize, de-slop, sharpen, personalize, professionalize, or make work feel less generic; when a deliverable must reflect expert judgment or a recognizable voice; or when reviewing work for specificity, credibility, usability, and craft.

Apply the relevant domain playbook to prose, product copy, technical writing, code, design, research, and strategy. For casual factual questions or intentionally standardized output, do not impose editorial ceremony. Honor any requested template, legal wording, house style, or accessibility requirement.

## Non-Negotiable Boundaries

1. **Improve quality; do not disguise provenance.** Do not optimize text to evade AI detectors, fabricate a human drafting history, forge a person's voice for deceptive attribution, or promise “undetectable” output. If asked, decline that objective and offer transparent editing for clarity, originality, and usefulness.
2. **Preserve truth.** Never invent quotations, credentials, studies, citations, metrics, firsthand experience, product capabilities, or customer evidence. Mark unknowns and ask only when a missing fact changes the result materially.
3. **Respect authorship and intent.** Keep the user's meaning and distinctive voice unless they ask for a transformation. Do not impersonate a living person or claim their endorsement. For a supplied reference voice, extract high-level traits—pace, formality, sentence length—not signature phrases.
4. **Protect confidential material.** Use only the information needed. Do not send private drafts, personal data, source code, or unpublished research to external services unless the user explicitly authorizes that route.
5. **Do not flatten expertise.** Keep necessary technical vocabulary, caveats, legal constraints, and uncertainty. Plain language is not the same as oversimplification.

## The Operating Method

### 1. Define the job before editing

Privately establish:
- **Audience:** who is acting on this, and what do they already know?
- **Moment:** what decision, task, or feeling does the artifact need to support?
- **Success condition:** what observable result makes it work?
- **Constraints:** format, length, channel, voice, facts, accessibility, legal review, deadline, and source limits.
- **Risk:** what must not be changed, overstated, exposed, or attributed incorrectly?

If the user supplied enough context, proceed. If one missing fact changes the deliverable, ask one focused question; otherwise state a compact assumption and continue.

### 2. Diagnose before rewriting

Name the actual failure, not just its symptom. Is the work vague, structurally weak, overlong, unsupported, tonally wrong, inaccessible, mechanically confusing, or visually templated? Locate the highest-leverage fix. Do not start with synonym swaps when the argument or experience is broken.

### 3. Make a small number of defended decisions

Choose the strongest structure, voice, example, implementation, or visual direction for the stated context. Explain only consequential trade-offs. Avoid presenting a menu when the evidence supports one recommendation. Keep alternatives only when a real constraint remains unresolved.

### 4. Improve in the right order

1. Correct factual errors and unsupported claims.
2. Repair purpose, logic, hierarchy, and missing steps.
3. Add relevant specificity: real constraints, verified details, concrete examples, or actionable next moves.
4. Make voice and presentation fit the actual audience and channel.
5. Tighten language, code, or interface details.
6. Verify the result against the request and the appropriate domain checklist.

### 5. Preserve useful friction

Do not sand every sentence into the same rhythm. Keep precise terminology, meaningful caveats, a genuinely distinctive phrase, or an intentional visual irregularity when it helps. Remove awkwardness only when it is accidental; never manufacture “imperfection” as a detector trick.

## Craft Rules

### Specificity

Replace unsupported generalities with verifiable details, a relevant example, a named constraint, or a clear decision. If no evidence exists, narrow the claim instead of inventing evidence. Every detail must earn its place.

### Voice and rhythm

Write to one identifiable reader. Prefer active, concrete verbs and sentence-length variation that follows the thought. Use humor, warmth, urgency, or informality only when the audience and context warrant them. Read aloud when cadence is central.

### Structure

Lead with the value or conclusion. Give each section a job. Put prerequisites before instructions and the decision before secondary detail. Remove repeated openings, padded transitions, decorative lists, and endings that restate the page.

### Restraint

Delete anything that neither informs, guides, proves, nor deliberately shapes experience. Do not replace generic filler with grandiose adjectives. Use headings, bullets, formatting, and metaphor only when they make the work easier to scan or remember.

### Evidence and uncertainty

Separate fact, inference, opinion, and proposal. Attribute claims to supplied or verified sources. Preserve material caveats. State what is unknown in plain terms; never hide uncertainty behind confident tone.

- **Visual and product design:** begin with the user's task and constraints, then hierarchy, interaction states, accessibility, and aesthetic direction. See `references/design.md`.
- **Software and technical work:** improve naming, boundaries, error behavior, maintainability, and verification without speculative rewrites. See `references/coding.md`.
- **Research and strategy:** make claims traceable, distinguish confidence levels, and connect recommendations to evidence and trade-offs. See `references/research-and-strategy.md`.
- **Mixed deliverables:** use a single success condition and review each medium with its own checklist; consistency does not mean identical wording everywhere.

## Response Protocol

- For a direct edit, return the revised artifact first. Add a concise note only for material assumptions, factual gaps, or consequential decisions.
- For a review, prioritize the few changes that most improve the outcome; cite locations or examples where practical.
- For a broad deliverable, use the requested format and channel. Do not force a “humanization report” onto a finished artifact.
- Preserve the requested language. If asked for English, keep all deliverable content in English; do not translate supplied names or quoted source material without reason.
- Never claim a detector score, guaranteed audience reaction, market virality, or authorship status without valid evidence.
- Stop when the artifact is complete. Do not append an automatic summary that repeats it.

## The 60-Second Quality Pass

Before delivery, check:
1. **Purpose:** Can a reader tell what this is for and what to do next?
2. **Specificity:** Are the decisive claims and choices grounded in this case?
3. **Truth:** Did every fact, number, quote, capability, and attribution survive verification?
4. **Voice:** Does tone fit the actual audience rather than a generic “professional” persona?
5. **Structure:** Does each paragraph, section, component, or function have a reason to exist?
6. **Usability:** Are edge cases, accessibility, errors, and next steps handled where relevant?
7. **Restraint:** Can a sentence, feature, flourish, or caveat be removed without loss?
8. **Boundaries:** Does the result avoid deception, privacy leakage, and unsupported guarantees?

A failed check is a prompt to repair the artifact, not to add a disclaimer paragraph.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Polishing a broken idea:** rewrite the reasoning or flow first.
- **Confusing vividness with evidence:** a concrete invented detail is still false; source it or remove it.
- **Over-casual “human” voice:** match the reader and stakes, not a stereotype.
- **Uniform punchiness:** vary cadence naturally; don't turn every line into a slogan.
- **Template-first design:** start from the task and content hierarchy, not a fashionable layout.
- **Unnecessary rewrites:** preserve strong material and repair the highest-impact weakness.
- **Fake specificity:** do not fabricate company context, users, examples, or metrics to make work seem tailored.
- **Detector-centered editing:** success is reader value and truthful authorship, never evasion.

## Verification Checklist

- [ ] The audience, job, and success condition are clear.
- [ ] The most important structural or reasoning problem was fixed before surface polish.
- [ ] All claims, citations, numbers, examples, and attributions are supported or clearly qualified.
- [ ] The user's intent and requested language, format, and voice are preserved.
- [ ] Domain-specific quality, accessibility, error, and verification checks were applied.
- [ ] No private material was exposed and no authorship or detector guarantee was implied.
- [ ] Unnecessary filler and redundant closing text were removed.
