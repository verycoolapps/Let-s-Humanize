# Writing and Editorial Playbook

## Start with reader, task, and claim

Before revising prose, write down (mentally or in a brief) the reader, what they need to understand or do, and the one claim or action the piece must land. If a sentence does not support that job, challenge it—even if it sounds polished.

## Repair the argument before the line

Use this order:

1. State the main point in one plain sentence.
2. Check that evidence supports it and that caveats are proportionate.
3. Put ideas in the order the reader needs them.
4. Give each paragraph one clear function: claim, evidence, explanation, transition, or action.
5. Edit wording, rhythm, and mechanics.

A stronger verb cannot repair a missing premise. Do not preserve a weak structure just because the original has elegant sentences.

## Build credible specificity

Prefer the exact audience, product constraint, verified example, source, or next step over vague appeals to “everyone.” Use names, dates, counts, quotations, and performance claims only when the source supports them. If a concrete example is illustrative rather than factual, label it as an example. When evidence is absent, narrow the sentence (“may help”) or remove it; never write a plausible statistic to make copy persuasive.

## Voice, not costume

Describe voice with usable traits: direct or reflective, formal or conversational, technical or plainspoken, dry or warm. Preserve memorable user phrasing if it works. For an unknown brand, choose a restrained default rather than inventing a personality. Never impersonate a real person or pass edited text off as their words without authorization.

## Shape the page

- Lead with the decision, value, or answer; add context only if the reader needs it.
- Use headings that promise actual content, not generic labels like “Overview” repeated everywhere.
- Keep paragraphs focused. Break them when the idea changes, not at an artificial fixed sentence count.
- Use lists for parallel items or steps; use prose for connected reasoning.
- Define specialized terms at first use when the reader may not know them.
- End on the final useful fact, decision, or action. Do not append a summary that only repeats the page.

## Cadence and editing

Read aloud for rhythm when voice matters. Vary sentence length because the ideas vary, not to imitate a statistical signature. Remove redundant transitions, inflated claims, throat-clearing, boilerplate disclaimers, and repeated conclusions. Keep a technical caveat if omitting it would mislead. Keep a sentence that carries real voice even if it is not mechanically symmetrical.

## Ethical boundaries

Do not rewrite to defeat AI detection, claim a person drafted text they did not draft, fabricate citations or lived experience, or mimic an identifiable living writer's signature style. Offer ordinary transparent editing for clarity, originality, accessibility, and a person's own voice. Never guarantee that a detector, search engine, audience, or publisher will respond in a particular way.

## Quick review

- Can the reader state the point after one pass?
- Does each claim have the evidence it needs?
- Is the tone right for this reader and channel?
- Are examples real, sourced, or clearly marked as illustrative?
- Can any paragraph or sentence be removed without loss?
- Does the ending stop at the right moment?

## Worked repair

**Before:** “Our revolutionary platform unlocks seamless growth for every modern business.”

**Diagnosis:** No mechanism, no evidence, and an unsupported universal promise.

**After (only if supported by product facts):** “Send a payment link in WhatsApp and track whether the customer has paid.”

The second version names a user action and observable outcome. If the product cannot do that, remove the claim rather than polishing it.

## Exercise

Take one paragraph from a real draft. Highlight the main claim, underline its evidence, and label every other sentence by its job. Remove or rewrite anything that has no job. Check that all concrete details can be traced to a source or are clearly marked as hypothetical.

## Further reading

- William Zinsser, *On Writing Well* (clarity, simplicity, voice).
- Joseph M. Williams and Joseph Bizup, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* (reader-centered prose).
- Purdue OWL, “Tone in Business Writing”: https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/subject_specific_writing/professional_technical_writing/tone_in_business_writing/index.html

Use these as craft references, not rigid house rules. The reader, evidence, and assignment govern.
